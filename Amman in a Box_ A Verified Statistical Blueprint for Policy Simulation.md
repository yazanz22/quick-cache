# Amman in a Box: A Verified Statistical Blueprint for Policy Simulation

## Demographics and Household Structure

A foundational understanding of Amman's demographic structure is essential for constructing a synthetic population model. This section synthesizes verified public statistics on total population, its geographic distribution across districts, age and sex composition, and household characteristics. The analysis prioritizes data from the Greater Amman Municipality (GAM), the Department of Statistics (DoS), and the 2015 General Census of Population and Housing, which provides the most granular and authoritative snapshot of the city's makeup [[37](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amman)]. While more recent estimates exist, they often lack the district-level detail necessary for a sophisticated simulation, making the 2015 census the primary anchor for this section. National figures from the DoS serve as reliable proxies where local data is unavailable or outdated [[26](https://www.facebook.com/thejordantimes/posts/the-department-of-statistics-dos-launched-the-2026-population-and-housing-census/1508181948023327/), [34](https://www.facebook.com/thejordantimes/posts/director-general-of-the-department-of-statistics-dos-haidar-fraihat-said-jordans/1482363837271805/)].

The total population of the Amman Governorate presents a notable discrepancy across sources, reflecting different estimation methods and years. One source cites a 2024 figure of 4.92 million people, representing 42% of Jordan's total population and underscoring the city's primate status [[36](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0264275126001484)]. Another report states the governorate's population was 4.5 million [[38](https://unhabitat.org/sites/default/files/2022/04/220411-final_amman_profile.pdf)]. However, the most specific and verifiable figure comes from the Greater Amman Municipality's (GAM) official spatial profile, which relies directly on the 2015 General Census of Population and Housing. According to this authoritative source, the total population of GAM was 4,007,526 as recorded in the 2015 census [[37](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amman)]. This figure serves as the baseline for the synthetic dataset, acknowledging that the population has likely grown since 2015 but without a more recent comprehensive count, it remains the most accurate available starting point. The rapid growth of the metropolis is further evidenced by historical population figures, which show a jump from 2.84 million in 2010 to 4.01 million in 2015 [[37](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amman)].

For policy simulation, understanding how this population is distributed across Amman's administrative districts is critical. The GAM provides a complete and invaluable table detailing the 22 administrative areas of the municipality, along with their area in square kilometers and their population as recorded in the 2015 census [[37](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amman)]. This data directly addresses the need for district-level population counts for key areas such as Basman, Marka, Sweileh, Abdali, Jabal Al-Hussein, and others. For instance, the central district of Basman had a 2015 population of 373,981, while the southern district of Wadi Al Seer had 241,830 [[37](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amman)]. These precise figures allow for the creation of a geographically representative sample of 800 residents, ensuring that more populous districts like Basman and Kherbet Al-Souk (population 186,158) are proportionally represented more than smaller ones like Uhod (population 40,000) [[37](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amman)]. The age distribution of Amman's population is another critical parameter for the synthetic model. The 2015 General Census provides a comprehensive breakdown of the population by single-year age groups, which can be aggregated into the user-specified bands [[37](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amman)]. This allows for the construction of a highly detailed age pyramid for the city. For example, the census data shows that a significant portion of the population is young; a third of Jordanian household members are under the age of 15, indicating a high dependency ratio [[39](https://qazinform.com/news/jordan-unveils-2023-population-and-family-health-survey-findings-a2a648)]. The total fertility rate has decreased to 2.6 children per woman, though it remains higher in rural areas than in urban centers like Amman (2.6) [[39](https://qazinform.com/news/jordan-unveils-2023-population-and-family-health-survey-findings-a2a648)]. The median age at first marriage is 22.5 for women and 27.5 for men, which influences demographic trends related to family formation [[39](https://qazinform.com/news/jordan-unveils-2023-population-and-family-health-survey-findings-a2a648)]. While these national-level indicators provide context, the 2015 GAM census data offers the precise age-band distribution required for the simulation [[37](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amman)]. The sex ratio in Jordan is consistently reported as being skewed towards males, with a national figure of 53% male and 47% female [[26](https://www.facebook.com/thejordantimes/posts/the-department-of-statistics-dos-launched-the-2026-population-and-housing-census/1508181948023327/), [34](https://www.facebook.com/thejordantimes/posts/director-general-of-the-department-of-statistics-dos-haidar-fraihat-said-jordans/1482363837271805/)]. This same ratio should be applied to Amman unless district-specific data indicates otherwise. Unfortunately, no specific data regarding the sex ratio for the 65+ age group in Amman was found in the provided sources.

A defining characteristic of modern Amman is its large non-Jordanian population, primarily composed of refugees and migrants. The 2015 census provides a definitive figure for this segment, stating that the total non-Jordanian population in Amman was 1,452,693 [[37](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amman)]. When compared to the total 2015 GAM population of 4,007,526, this represents a substantial share of approximately 36.2%. This makes Amman not just a capital city, but also a major hub for displaced populations. The census data further breaks down these communities by nationality, providing valuable information for simulating diversity. The largest groups were Syrians (435,578), Egyptians (390,631), and Palestinians (308,091) [[37](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amman)]. Other significant groups included Iraqis (121,893) and Yemenis (27,109), with a residual category for other nationalities totaling 147,742 [[37](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amman)]. This detailed breakdown is crucial for creating a realistic synthetic population that reflects Amman's complex social fabric.

Finally, this section covers household structure, including size and composition. The Department of Statistics consistently reports that the average household size in Jordan is 4.8 members [[26](https://www.facebook.com/thejordantimes/posts/the-department-of-statistics-dos-launched-the-2026-population-and-housing-census/1508181948023327/), [34](https://www.facebook.com/thejordantimes/posts/director-general-of-the-department-of-statistics-dos-haidar-fraihat-said-jordans/1482363837271805/), [39](https://qazinform.com/news/jordan-unveils-2023-population-and-family-health-survey-findings-a2a648)]. This figure serves as a robust national average and should be used as the baseline for average household size in the Amman-based synthetic dataset. In terms of household composition, the DoS notes that women head 15% of households [[39](https://qazinform.com/news/jordan-unveils-2023-population-and-family-health-survey-findings-a2a648)]. However, two critical pieces of information requested were not found in the provided sources: the share of households with a member aged 65+, and the share of elderly people living alone or with only a spouse. While Amman has the lowest poverty rate of all governorates, it is home to the largest number of poor individuals due to the concentration of the population [[45](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/entities/publication/f2463016-8a40-58b2-927a-d5cad744fcc7)], and the national poverty line in Amman is set at JD 703 [[49](https://books.openedition.org/ifpo/5036?lang=en)]. This highlights the coexistence of wealth and deprivation within the city's household structure, even if specific compositional data for the elderly is unavailable.

## Disability and Functional Difficulties

Understanding the prevalence and nature of disability in Amman is crucial for designing inclusive policies and services. This section analyzes public statistics on disability, focusing on the methodology used to measure it, overall prevalence, distribution by age and sex, and the specific types of functional difficulties experienced by the population. A central challenge in this domain is the existence of conflicting prevalence figures in Jordan, which stems from different survey instruments and definitions. The user's explicit instruction to prioritize data derived from the Washington Group on Disability Statistics (WG) questions provides the key to resolving this ambiguity and selecting the most appropriate and internationally comparable metric for the synthetic population model [[21](https://unstats.un.org/unsd/methodology/citygroups/washington.cshtml)].

The most significant finding is the stark contrast between two widely cited disability prevalence rates in Jordan. One source reports a rate of 2.7% based on the 2015 Population and Housing Census using traditional questions [[18](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/343306541_The_current_situation_of_persons_with_disabilities_in_Jordan)]. In contrast, multiple other sources, including reports from Handicap International and the Higher Council for Persons with Disabilities, cite a much higher prevalence of 11.2% for the overall Jordanian population aged five and older, also referencing the 2015 census [[17](https://www.hi.org/sn_uploads/document/Briefing-Paper_Jordan-Disability-Social-Protection_September-2023_Final-Version.pdf), [20](https://www.hpc.org.jo/en/content/jordan-joins-world-celebrating-international-day-persons-disabilities), [22](https://assets.ctfassets.net/brrac8t1yx20/5MlyEMdh2lnPk24ooXH5bc/a5f7f5519c5372ff9ce1a2e551ff15a5/SUPPORTING_PERSONS_WITH_DISABILITY_IN_JORDAN_-_FINAL.pdf)]. This discrepancy is not an error but a reflection of differing measurement methodologies. The 11.2% figure is derived from an interpretation of the 2015 census data through the lens of the Washington Group Short Set on Functioning (WG-SS), a standardized tool designed to produce internationally comparable disability statistics [[21](https://unstats.un.org/unsd/methodology/citygroups/washington.cshtml), [75](https://www.disabilitydatainitiative.org/ds-e-methods/)]. The WG-SS defines disability as having "a lot of difficulty...or cannot do" a core activity, a threshold intended to identify individuals at risk of social exclusion due to environmental barriers [[24](https://www.washingtongroup-disability.com/wg-blog/post/differences-in-reported-disability-prevalence-rates-is-something-wrong-if-i-dont-get-15-120/)]. Given the user's directive to use Washington Group questions, the 11.2% prevalence rate is the correct and most suitable figure to use for the synthetic dataset. It is important to note that the World Bank has highlighted this methodological distinction, noting that different statistical approaches can yield vastly different results, with some including individuals with "some difficulty" while the WG approach focuses on those with "a lot of difficulty" or inability [[24](https://www.washingtongroup-disability.com/wg-blog/post/differences-in-reported-disability-prevalence-rates-is-something-wrong-if-i-dont-get-15-120/)]. The choice of 11.2% therefore aligns with the standard for identifying functional limitations relevant to policy and service design.

The prevalence of disability increases dramatically with age, a pattern clearly documented in the available data. While the overall Jordanian population has a disability rate of 11.2%, this figure rises to 49.3% for the population aged 65 and over [[23](https://arabstates.unfpa.org/sites/default/files/pub-pdf/country_profile_-_jordan_10-1-2024.pdf)]. This is a powerful demographic insight that must be incorporated into the synthetic model. It implies that nearly half of all elderly individuals in Jordan experience significant functional difficulties, which has profound implications for healthcare, social support, and accessibility planning. The 2015 census data also revealed that only 16.1% of persons with disabilities were employed, compared to 36.6% in the general population, highlighting the economic marginalization faced by this group [[19](https://www.academia.edu/43952639/The_current_situation_of_persons_with_disabilities_in_Jordan)]. This low employment rate is attributed to societal stigma, inadequate vocational training, and cultural barriers to hiring [[19](https://www.academia.edu/43952639/The_current_situation_of_persons_with_disabilities_in_Jordan)]. Furthermore, women with disabilities face compounded discrimination, with a reported illiteracy rate of 40% among them [[19](https://www.academia.edu/43952639/The_current_situation_of_persons_with_disabilities_in_Jordan)]. No specific data on disability prevalence by sex for Amman was found, so the national figure of 11.7% for Jordanian males and 10.8% for Jordanian females would be used as a proxy [[20](https://www.hpc.org.jo/en/content/jordan-joins-world-celebrating-international-day-persons-disabilities)].

To create a nuanced synthetic population, it is necessary to understand the specific types of difficulties people experience. Based on the 2015 census data, the most common challenges reported in Jordan are difficulty seeing (9% of the population), followed by difficulty walking or climbing stairs (7%), and difficulty remembering or concentrating (4%) [[39](https://qazinform.com/news/jordan-unveils-2023-population-and-family-health-survey-findings-a2a648)]. The Washington Group Short Set, which underpins the 11.2% prevalence figure, covers six domains: seeing, hearing, walking, cognition, self-care, and communication [[21](https://unstats.un.org/unsd/methodology/citygroups/washington.cshtml), [75](https://www.disabilitydatainitiative.org/ds-e-methods/)]. The available data confirms that seeing and walking are the most prevalent issues. This information allows for the assignment of specific types of functional limitations to individuals designated as having a disability in the model. For instance, a synthetic individual could be assigned a "seeing" difficulty with a certain probability derived from the 9% figure, and a "walking" difficulty with a probability derived from the 7% figure, recognizing that these can overlap.

A critical limitation in the available data is the lack of a specific statistic for severe mobility difficulty, defined as "cannot walk at all" or experiencing "a lot of difficulty." The 7% prevalence of walking difficulty includes both mild and severe cases [[39](https://qazinform.com/news/jordan-unveils-2023-population-and-family-health-survey-findings-a2a648)]. Similarly, no direct figure was found for the number of wheelchair users in Jordan. This represents a significant gap for modeling accessibility needs. The closest available proxy is the 7% walking difficulty rate, which would encompass the target population but lacks specificity. Therefore, when populating the synthetic dataset, this variable would be marked with caution, acknowledging that the true number of individuals with severe mobility impairments is unknown from the provided sources. ## Transport Patterns and Infrastructure

Transportation patterns in Amman are characterized by a heavy reliance on private vehicles, significant daily congestion, and a developing but underutilized public transport network. This section compiles verified statistics on car ownership, driving licenses, main modes of transport, commute times, and public transport fares to build a realistic model of mobility in the city. The data, drawn from municipal plans, academic studies, and government sources, reveals a transportation system strained by rapid urbanization and a population where half its residents reportedly use their own cars within the city boundaries [[4](https://opentransportationjournal.com/VOLUME/13/PAGE/227/FULLTEXT/)].

The dominance of private cars is a defining feature of life in Amman. A 2017 survey found that private vehicles were the primary mode of transport for 64.4% of respondents [[5](https://www.mdpi.com/2413-8851/7/4/111)]. This is corroborated by a statement from the Jordanian Department of Statistics that approximately half of the city's estimated 4 million people use their own cars within the city limits [[4](https://opentransportationjournal.com/VOLUME/13/PAGE/227/FULLTEXT/)]. This high level of car dependency contributes significantly to traffic congestion, with an estimated 1,200,000 cars entering the city daily, and around 500,000 vehicles present during the morning peak hour [[4](https://opentransportationjournal.com/VOLUME/13/PAGE/227/FULLTEXT/)]. The share of households owning at least one private car is not explicitly stated in the provided documents, but the overwhelming preference for private vehicles strongly suggests a high ownership rate. Data on car ownership by income quintile is also not available in the provided sources. However, the link between car ownership and driving licenses is well-established: among participants who chose private vehicles in a survey, 93.2% held a driving license [[5](https://www.mdpi.com/2413-8851/7/4/111)]. This is a crucial piece of information for linking vehicle access to driver availability in the synthetic model.

In contrast to the dominance of cars, public transport serves a much smaller fraction of the population. Daily ridership for all public transport modes (buses, mini-buses, and taxis) was reported to be 500,000 passengers [[4](https://opentransportationjournal.com/VOLUME/13/PAGE/227/FULLTEXT/)]. This is dwarfed by the number of potential commuters using private vehicles. The purpose of these trips is also telling: a 2017 survey of public transport users identified educational trips as the most common reason (35%), followed by work-related trips (30%) [[4](https://opentransportationjournal.com/VOLUME/13/PAGE/227/FULLTEXT/)]. The main user categories were students (31%) and public sector employees (25%) [[4](https://opentransportationjournal.com/VOLUME/13/PAGE/227/FULLTEXT/)]. Despite its importance for these segments, the system faces significant challenges. A study conducted in 2017 recorded average waiting times of 25 minutes and 25 seconds for buses during peak hours and 22 minutes and 2 seconds for mini-buses [[4](https://opentransportationjournal.com/VOLUME/13/PAGE/227/FULLTEXT/)]. These long waits represent a significant cost to users, estimated to be up to 12% of their monthly income, equivalent to about US$80 [[4](https://opentransportationjournal.com/VOLUME/13/PAGE/227/FULLTEXT/)]. This highlights a major inefficiency in the current system that a policy simulation could aim to address.

Recognizing these challenges, Amman is developing a Bus Rapid Transit (BRT) system, which is expected to increase the public transport mode share from 14% to 40% by 2025 [[2](https://www.c40.org/case-studies/bus-rapid-transit-to-tackle-air-pollution-co2-emissions-and-improve-mass-public-transportation/)]. The BRT system launched in 2021 and operates on dedicated lanes, featuring improved amenities like surveillance cameras and free Wi-Fi [[3](https://www.amideastedabroad.org/home/how-to-use-the-bus)]. The BRT currently has two main routes, one from Sweileh to Ras Al-Ain and another from Sweileh to Mahatta, with a third route connecting to Zarqa launched in May 2024 [[1](https://www.expatfocus.com/jordan/guide/jordan-buses-and-trams)]. In 2024, the combined BRT and Amman Bus systems carried over 29 million passengers, indicating a growing role in meeting the city's transport demands [[1](https://www.expatfocus.com/jordan/guide/jordan-buses-and-trams)]. To encourage usage, the BRT system is integrated with other transport modes, which increased the willingness to use it from 30% to 70% in one study [[5](https://www.mdpi.com/2413-8851/7/4/111)].

Official regulated taxi fares are not explicitly detailed in the provided documents, but informal minibus journeys are estimated to cost between 0.5 and 2 JOD [[1](https://www.expatfocus.com/jordan/guide/jordan-buses-and-trams)]. ## Digital Access and E-Government Adoption

Amman exhibits near-universal penetration of basic digital infrastructure, yet a significant gap persists between connectivity and the adoption of advanced digital services. This section examines verified statistics on internet and smartphone access, digital literacy levels, and the use of e-government platforms in Amman. The data, primarily sourced from the Ministry of Digital Economy and Entrepreneurship (MoDEE) and the Department of Statistics, reveals a population that is highly connected but not uniformly digitally skilled or engaged with online public services. This nuanced picture is critical for simulating the potential impact of digital transformation initiatives.

Access to digital devices and the internet in Amman is exceptionally high. According to the 2024 Household Use and Penetration of Communications and Information Technology Survey by MoDEE, the percentage of households with home internet service in Amman exceeded 98%, surpassing the national average of 96.5% [[51](https://jordan.gov.jo/EN/NewsDetails/Ministry_of_Digital_Economy_and_Entrepreneurship_Announces_Results_of_the_2024_Household_ICT_Usage_and_Access_Survey), [52](https://petra.gov.jo/en/news/household-internet-access-in-jordan-reached-965-in-2024-survey-reveals), [54](https://jordantimes.com/news/local/965-of-jordanian-households-connected-to-internet-data)]. At the individual level, internet usage in Jordan rose to 95.6% in 2024, up from 92.5% in 2023 [[51](https://jordan.gov.jo/EN/NewsDetails/Ministry_of_Digital_Economy_and_Entrepreneurship_Announces_Results_of_the_2024_Household_ICT_Usage_and_Access_Survey), [52](https://petra.gov.jo/en/news/household-internet-access-in-jordan-reached-965-in-2024-survey-reveals)]. Smartphone ownership is similarly widespread, reaching 99% of households in Amman, the highest rate among all governorates [[51](https://jordan.gov.jo/EN/NewsDetails/Ministry_of_Digital_Economy_and_Entrepreneurship_Announces_Results_of_the_2024_Household_ICT_Usage_and_Access_Survey), [52](https://petra.gov.jo/en/news/household-internet-access-in-jordan-reached-965-in-2024-survey-reveals), [54](https://jordantimes.com/news/local/965-of-jordanian-households-connected-to-internet-data)]. Nationally, smartphone ownership stood at 97.5% in 2024 [[54](https://jordantimes.com/news/local/965-of-jordanian-households-connected-to-internet-data)]. This near-complete saturation of basic connectivity means that barriers to accessing digital services are less about hardware availability and more about skills, awareness, and trust.

However, the picture becomes more complex when examining digital literacy. Self-reported skills indicate proficiency in basic tasks but a decline in more advanced competencies. The 2024 survey found that 79.6% of individuals could copy and paste, and 64.1% could download software [[51](https://jordan.gov.jo/EN/NewsDetails/Ministry_of_Digital_Economy_and_Entrepreneurship_Announces_Results_of_the_2024_Household_ICT_Usage_and_Access_Survey), [52](https://petra.gov.jo/en/news/household-internet-access-in-jordan-reached-965-in-2024-survey-reveals)]. However, only 32.4% reported being able to prepare electronic presentations, a more advanced skill [[51](https://jordan.gov.jo/EN/NewsDetails/Ministry_of_Digital_Economy_and_Entrepreneurship_Announces_Results_of_the_2024_Household_ICT_Usage_and_Access_Survey), [52](https://petra.gov.jo/en/news/household-internet-access-in-jordan-reached-965-in-2024-survey-reveals)]. This suggests a spectrum of digital capability within the population. The survey also found that computer ownership in Jordanian households increased to 42.4% in 2024, with laptops being the most common type (34.3%), followed by tablets (21.6%) and desktops (15.2%) [[51](https://jordan.gov.jo/EN/NewsDetails/Ministry_of_Digital_Economy_and_Entrepreneurship_Announces_Results_of_the_2024_Household_ICT_Usage_and_Access_Survey), [52](https://petra.gov.jo/en/news/household-internet-access-in-jordan-reached-965-in-2024-survey-reveals)]. Computer ownership is also broken down by age, showing that younger individuals are more likely to own and use computers. For example, 42.4% of all individuals in Jordan reported using computers [[52](https://petra.gov.jo/en/news/household-internet-access-in-jordan-reached-965-in-2024-survey-reveals)]. This data allows for the simulation of different tiers of digital literacy among the synthetic population, moving beyond a simple binary of "connected" or "not connected."

Despite this high level of connectivity, the adoption of e-government services remains relatively low. Only 38.1% of individuals in Jordan reported having accessed at least one e-government service [[51](https://jordan.gov.jo/EN/NewsDetails/Ministry_of_Digital_Economy_and_Entrepreneurship_Announces_Results_of_the_2024_Household_ICT_Usage_and_Access_Survey), [52](https://petra.gov.jo/en/news/household-internet-access-in-jordan-reached-965-in-2024-survey-reveals), [54](https://jordantimes.com/news/local/965-of-jordanian-households-connected-to-internet-data)]. This "adoption gap" is a critical finding, suggesting that factors beyond simple access—such as user interface design, perceived security, language, or convenience—are significant barriers to uptake. The success of specific applications, however, points to pathways for improvement. The "Sanad" application, Jordan's national digital identity platform, has seen remarkable growth, reaching 2.5 million users and activating 3.25 million digital identities [[60](https://www.jordanpulse.com/article/13994), [61](https://rene.cx/de/words/jordans-sanad-app-hits-3-25-million-digital-id-activations)]. This demonstrates that targeted, high-value services can drive significant user engagement. Users of e-government services report overwhelmingly positive experiences, with 98.9% finding services easy to use, 99.0% confirming fast delivery, and 99.3% considering them fully digital [[51](https://jordan.gov.jo/EN/NewsDetails/Ministry_of_Digital_Economy_and_Entrepreneurship_Announces_Results_of_the_2024_Household_ICT_Usage_and_Access_Survey), [52](https://petra.gov.jo/en/news/household-internet-access-in-jordan-reached-965-in-2024-survey-reveals)]. This indicates that the technology itself is mature and effective; the challenge lies in promoting its use and expanding the range of available services.

## Labor Market and Economic Indicators

The labor market in Jordan, and particularly in the capital Amman, is characterized by high unemployment, especially among youth and women, and a large informal economy. Economic indicators such as household income and poverty rates reveal significant disparities. This section compiles verified statistics on labor force participation, unemployment, income distribution, and poverty to inform a socioeconomic dimension of the synthetic population model. The primary sources for this analysis are the Department of Statistics' Labor Force Survey and the Household Expenditure and Income Survey (HEIS).

Labor force participation and unemployment rates in Jordan exhibit a stark gender disparity. According to the World Bank, the labor force participation rate among females is 16%, compared to 62.5% for males [[31](https://genderdata.worldbank.org/en/economies/jordan)]. This dramatic difference highlights deep-seated structural and social barriers to female workforce entry. Unemployment rates reflect a similar pattern. In late 2024, the overall unemployment rate for Jordanians was 21% [[29](https://theforum.erf.org.eg/2025/02/24/a-macroeconomic-accounting-of-unemployment-in-jordan-unemployment-is-mainly-an-issue-for-adults-and-men/)]. However, this masks significant differences by gender: the rate for Jordanian men was 19%, while for Jordanian women it was 31% [[29](https://theforum.erf.org.eg/2025/02/24/a-macroeconomic-accounting-of-unemployment-in-jordan-unemployment-is-mainly-an-issue-for-adults-and-men/)]. Youth unemployment is particularly acute. For the 15-24 age group, the unemployment rate reached an alarming 46% in late 2024, with rates of 40% for young men and 66% for young women [[29](https://theforum.erf.org.eg/2025/02/24/a-macroeconomic-accounting-of-unemployment-in-jordan-unemployment-is-mainly-an-issue-for-adults-and-men/)]. These figures underscore the challenges faced by young people and women in securing stable employment. The most recent available data for these indicators appears to be from late 2024 or early 2025 [[29](https://theforum.erf.org.eg/2025/02/24/a-macroeconomic-accounting-of-unemployment-in-jordan-unemployment-is-mainly-an-issue-for-adults-and-men/), [31](https://genderdata.worldbank.org/en/economies/jordan)]. As specific Amman governorate data for these indicators is not available in the provided sources, these national figures should be used as a proxy for the city's labor market conditions, with the caveat that Amman's status as the economic hub may result in slightly better outcomes than the national average.

The structure of work in Jordan is also noteworthy. The informal employment rate for adults (ages 25+) was 52.4% in 2022, meaning that more than half of the adult workforce operates outside the formal, regulated economy [[28](https://humancapital.worldbank.org/en/economy/JOR)]. This rate is even higher for youth, at 59% [[28](https://humancapital.worldbank.org/en/economy/JOR)]. Among university-educated individuals in Jordan, the unemployment rate was 26% in late 2024, indicating that higher education does not guarantee employment [[29](https://theforum.erf.org.eg/2025/02/24/a-macroeconomic-accounting-of-unemployment-in-jordan-unemployment-is-mainly-an-issue-for-adults-and-men/)]. The typical working week in the public sector consists of five days, from Sunday to Thursday . Private sector average weekly hours are not specified in the provided documents, but the public sector structure provides a baseline for working days. The share of workers in shift work or manual labor is also not available in the provided sources. These gaps limit the ability to model occupational diversity in detail.

On the economic side, household income distribution and poverty are central to understanding living standards. The HEIS is the primary database for tracking poverty and living standards in Jordan [[40](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/469111551866278210/pdf/Jordan-HEIS-201718-Completion-Note.pdf), [44](https://documents.worldbank.org/en/publication/documents-reports/documentdetail/469111551866278210)]. Fieldwork for the most recent survey was conducted between November 2021 and October 2022, covering 25,000 households [[41](https://www.dst.dk/ext/38520110439/0/jordan2022/Mission-3-1-2-An-Overview-of-The-Household-Expenditure-and-Income-Survey-(HEIS)-(ENG)--pdf)]. While the final published results for 2021/2022 were announced in early 2023 [[43](https://en.ammonnews.net/article/58592)], detailed microdata is often not publicly released. Therefore, for the purpose of building the synthetic dataset, the shape of the income distribution from this survey can be used as a template, supplemented by available summary statistics. The national poverty rate has been on an upward trend, increasing from 14.4% in 2010 to 22.2% in 2017, and reaching 24% in 2021 according to one report [[46](https://armgpublishing.com/journals/sec/volume-4-issue-4/article-4/), [50](https://www.jordannews.jo/Section-109/News/More-than-one-third-of-Jordanians-live-below-poverty-line-report-finds-29697)]. However, poverty is not evenly distributed across governorates. Amman has the lowest poverty rate of any governorate, at 8.3% [[48](https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/vu-my/wp-content/uploads/sites/666/2013/04/14102327/Country-Fact-Sheet-Jordan-OCHA.pdf)]. Yet, due to its large population, Amman is home to the largest number of poor individuals in the country [[45](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/entities/publication/f2463016-8a40-58b2-927a-d5cad744fcc7)]. The absolute poverty line in Jordan varies by governorate, and it is highest in Amman at JD 703 per month [[49](https://books.openedition.org/ifpo/5036?lang=en)]. This figure is a critical input for the model, allowing for the calculation of poverty status for households based on their simulated income. The national poverty line for the 2017-18 survey was set at JD 100 per month, but this was before the significant economic pressures of recent years [[50](https://www.jordannews.jo/Section-109/News/More-than-one-third-of-Jordanians-live-below-poverty-line-report-finds-29697)]. The most current and relevant figure for Amman is the JD 703 monthly threshold [[49](https://books.openedition.org/ifpo/5036?lang=en)].

## Personal Finance and Civil Status Service Metrics

This section covers two distinct but important areas for the synthetic population model: personal finance metrics, including loans and debt, and the operational details of the Civil Status and Passports Department (CSPD) in Amman, which serves as the public service for the simulation. The personal finance data, added at the user's request, provides context on indebtedness and borrowing capacity. The CSPD data outlines the parameters of the service being modeled, including fees, locations, and technological capabilities.

Personal finance in Jordan is characterized by significant household debt and strict lending criteria. The Central Bank of Jordan (CBJ) reported that total individual indebtedness owed to banks reached JD 11.8 billion, while total household credit market debt amounted to JD 14.312 billion in the first quarter of 2023 [[16](https://www.facebook.com/JordanNewsDaily/posts/individual-debt-reached-jd14312-billion-in-q1-2026-nearly-40-per-cent-of-total-b/1396065472631465/), [65](https://www.facebook.com/thejordantimes/posts/total-individual-indebtedness-in-jordan-reached-jd14312-billion-at-the-end-of-th/1485829293591926/)]. This represents a substantial financial obligation for the population, with household credit market debt as a proportion of disposable income reaching 176.4% in Q1 2023 [[16](https://www.facebook.com/JordanNewsDaily/posts/individual-debt-reached-jd14312-billion-in-q1-2026-nearly-40-per-cent-of-total-b/1396065472631465/)]. The official interest rate set by the CBJ, known as the Main Interest Rate, was 6.00% [[66](https://www.cbj.gov.jo/Default/EN/)]. However, advertised rates for consumer loans are typically higher. For example, a financial institution offers a personal loan with an announced interest rate starting from 9.08% for a 7-year term, a housing loan at 7.15%, and a salary overdraft service priced at 16.50% [[15](https://bankofjordan.com/en/products-services-tariff)]. These rates are based on the overnight interbank rate and are subject to commissions and other fees [[15](https://bankofjordan.com/en/products-services-tariff)]. A crucial constraint on borrowing capacity is the Debt Burden Ratio (DBR) imposed by banks. For most applicants, a borrower's total monthly loan payments cannot exceed 50% of their gross salary [[68](https://hbtf.com/en/retail/loans/personal-loans)]. For Social Security retirees, this limit can be as high as 60% [[68](https://hbtf.com/en/retail/loans/personal-loans)]. This DBR rule is a powerful and realistic constraint to implement in the simulation, as it directly links an individual's ability to take on new debt to their income level. The average loan amount for an individual is not specified in the provided documents, making it difficult to populate this variable with precision.

The second part of this section focuses on the Civil Status and Passports Department (CSPD), the entity whose services are the subject of the policy simulation. The CSPD provides clear and accessible information about its operations, which is vital for modeling the user journey. The official fee for renewing a Jordanian national ID card via the electronic service is JD 1 when paid through cash at a CSPD office [[8](https://www.cspd.gov.jo/EN/ListDetails/E__Services_/44/3)]. The process is designed for efficiency, with a processing time of 24 hours for the electronic service [[8](https://www.cspd.gov.jo/EN/ListDetails/E__Services_/44/3)]. The service can be accessed online through the CSPD's official website or the "Sanad" government application, offering a convenient alternative to visiting an office [[8](https://www.cspd.gov.jo/EN/ListDetails/E__Services_/44/3), [9](https://moi.gov.jo/En/NewsDetails/Launch_of_the_Jordanian_ePassport_Service)]. However, a critical piece of data for the simulation—the volume of online transactions for ID renewal—is NOT FOUND in the provided sources. While the government has ambitious goals to increase e-transactions from 4.63% to 26%, no actual transaction numbers for this specific service are available [[9](https://moi.gov.jo/En/NewsDetails/Launch_of_the_Jordanian_ePassport_Service), [10](https://jordantimes.com/news/local/prime-minister-inspects-civil-status-and-passports-department-stresses-expansion-of-digital-services)].

The main Head Office is centrally located at Tabarbour/Al Mashaghel Circle, next to the Public Security Directorate [[62](https://cspd.gov.jo/EN/ListDetails/Department_Directorates_and_Offices/17/1)]. There is also a secondary office in Jabal Amman at First Circle, behind the Iraqi Embassy [[62](https://cspd.gov.jo/EN/ListDetails/Department_Directorates_and_Offices/17/1)]. This distribution of offices is crucial for modeling service accessibility. Working hours for these offices are not fully detailed, but the department does offer evening and Saturday hours at some locations to accommodate citizens . The CSPD also employs mobile service units, deploying a mobile station to issue smart ID cards in various neighborhoods such as Disi, Tuwaisha, and Ram [[63](https://jordantimes.com/news/local/cspd-deploys-mobile-station-issue-smart-id-cards-southern-regions)].
